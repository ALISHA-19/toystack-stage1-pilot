Stage 1 Literature Search: My Approach to the Six-Week Pilot

Alisha ChaudhariProduct Manager / Client Implementation Partner take-home, Toystack AINov 2026
Key decisions in this plan
  • Evidence, not verdicts: every molecule links to the exact sentence or table it came from; the scientist decides, not an AI score.
  • Licensed sources only: free or commercially licensed public data; our lawyer confirms the licences in week 1.
  • One protein, end to end: working by week 2, full papers in week 3, client testing in week 5, demo in week 6.
  • The client's own test list is the yardstick: 20–30 molecules their team already knows, plus five measures agreed in week 1.
  • A clear cut list: BindingDB, screen polish and the approved-drug filter go first; links to sources and accuracy checks are never cut.

Plan of Action for Stage 1 (Literature Search)

1.Scope Defined

Today, a researcher picks a protein, searches databases and reads many papers to find molecules shown to act on it. This takes weeks, depends on who does the reading, and things get missed.

The VP asked to "type in a protein and get the best molecules back". In the same call, they said they don't trust AI summaries and want their team to see the evidence and decide. So Stage 1 should not pick the best molecule. It should find and organise the evidence, and the scientist decides.

What Stage 1 does: the researcher enters a protein and gets a shortlist of molecules. Each molecule shows its evidence, with a link to the exact sentence or table it came from.

For whom: the client's Senior Scientist and one or two colleagues working on the pilot protein. The VP is the sponsor. Whoever runs the simulation stage receives the output. I have not assumed Toystack already has a simulation tool; who owns that step is a week-1 question.

What the researcher does:

  1. Types the protein name and confirms the exact protein (for example, the human version or a specific mutation). Protein names are often ambiguous.
  2. Sees a shortlist. Each row shows the molecule, how strong the evidence is, how many sources support it, how recent it is, and whether it is an approved drug (the Senior Scientist asked about repurposing).
  3. Opens a molecule to see each piece of evidence: the sentence or table row, the paper, and whether it came from a curated database or was found by AI.
  4. Marks each molecule keep, reject or unsure, with a short note.
  5. Exports the kept molecules.

How results are sorted: by strength of evidence, not by an AI score.

  • Strong: a measured lab result for the molecule against this protein.
  • Medium: a paper says the molecule acts on the protein, but gives no number.
  • Weak: the two are only mentioned together. Hidden by default.

Lab results are only compared when they come from the same kind of test. Molecules named only as, for example, "compound 12b", with the structure shown in a drawing, go to a "needs checking" list, because the first version can't read drawings.

What it hands over: a file with each kept molecule's chemical structure, reported result, evidence links and the scientist's decision, plus a reference to the protein's 3D structure. The format is agreed in week 1 with whoever runs simulation.

The client calls this "protein–ligand matching" and our engineers say "target–hit matching". I would use the client's term, and avoid "AI-powered literature review" in this pilot, because it sounds like the AI summaries the VP doesn't trust.

I would follow Finance, because a pharma client will ask where every result came from. Each licence was checked on the source's own website on 3 November 2026 (links at the end). Later phases: patents through SureChEMBL [10] (CC BY 4.0); preprints from bioRxiv and medRxiv [11], where each author picks the licence, so only some can be used commercially; citation and retraction information from OpenAlex [12] (CC0; free API up to a daily limit, then paid).

Not using: Google Scholar has no official API, and Google's terms forbid automated searching [14]. DrugBank's free version is non-commercial only; commercial use needs a paid licence [13]. ChEMBL's approved-drug flag covers repurposing for the pilot. Paywalled papers can't be scraped. Conference posters have no reliable public source.

Open legal questions, for a lawyer by the end of week 1: can the AI read and quote short parts of copyrighted abstracts, and what does ChEMBL's "share-alike" condition mean if Toystack later sells this as a product? Until we have answers, we take the cautious option.

3.Integration Structure

How a search flows: the researcher confirms the protein (1). We collect records from the databases and papers (2) and find the passages about it (3). The AI records the molecule, the result and the exact quote in a fixed form (4). We match different names for the same molecule (5), store every item with its source and licence (6), show the shortlist for review (7), and export (8).

The quote check: if the AI's quote can't be found word for word in the paper, the item is thrown out. This stops invented evidence. The AI can still attach a real sentence to the wrong molecule, so we measure accuracy (Task 2).

Built for "any lab, any protein": leadership wants this for any lab and any protein, so I keep what is reusable apart from what is pilot-specific. Reusable: the source connections, AI extraction with the quote check, name matching, the evidence store with licences, the review screens and the export. Pilot-specific: the chosen protein and its test list, this client's export format, and their separate space. The aim is that a second protein needs no new code, and a new lab needs only its own space, test list and export format. New sources, such as patents or a client's own reports, each connect separately, so they can be added without rebuilding.

Client data: the pilot uses public data only and runs in our own space; nothing is installed on the client's side. The protein the client is working on is still treated as confidential: a separate space for this client, named users only, and an AI model set up not to keep what we send. Client IT only needs to approve the protein name leaving their network. I ask before kickoff, and if approval takes time we build on a public example protein meanwhile. Internal reports come in a later phase, processed inside the client's own systems.

4.The 1st Step

What I would do first (week 1): confirm the protein. Ask the Senior Scientist for 20–30 molecules they already know work on it (our "test list"), at least a third not yet in ChEMBL, so we test whether the AI finds anything new. Agree the success measures and the export format. Get the lawyer's answers.

What I would build first: one protein, start to finish. Confirm the protein, pull ChEMBL results, search papers, run the AI with the quote check, show a simple shortlist, allow keep or reject, and export. Working by the end of week 2, with rough screens but real data. This tests the biggest unknown early: can the AI find real evidence beyond the databases, accurately enough for scientists to trust it?

What I would leave out: patents, preprints, internal documents, posters, many proteins at once, chat or AI summaries, any AI verdict on whether a molecule is a good drug, and a direct connection to a simulation system (a file is enough for now).

How I would know it is working: the five measures in Task 2, checked against the client's own test list.

2.Dependencies & Risks

  • From the client: protein and test list by day 5 at the latest; about 3 hours a week from the Senior Scientist; two testers in week 5; IT's answer on whether the protein name may leave their network, before we start.
  • From the data: enough free full-text papers exist for the chosen protein. We check this in week 1.
  • Within the team: the two ML engineers are the tightest resource, so they get no side work. BE is fully loaded in weeks 2–3, which is why BindingDB is optional. The architect's days are booked in advance.

Critical risks to the date: full-text papers in week 3 taking longer than planned; client inputs arriving late; client IT approval; AI accuracy below target by week 5.

3.Tracking

Weekly sprints with a Friday demo; the Senior Scientist joins from week 2. A 15-minute stand-up each morning. One shared list of risks, open issues and client dependencies, each with an owner and a date. A Friday email to the VP: on track or at risk, what's done, latest numbers, decisions needed. A short call with the VP and CTO every two weeks.

Early warning signs: the first version isn't working by the end of week 2; accuracy is below 80% in week 3; a client input is more than two days late; work is two days behind plan. If any of these happens, I tell the CTO the same day and the VP within 24 hours, with options.

Email to the Client's VP of Research

Subject: Our six-week pilot: what we will deliver, and what we need from you

Dear Dr [Name],

Thank you for your team's time last week. As I understand it, your scientists spend weeks reading papers to find molecules worth pursuing. You want this to be faster, but your team needs to see the evidence and decide for themselves, not read an AI summary. Our plan is built around that.

In the first six weeks, we will deliver a working pilot for one protein. A scientist enters the protein and gets a shortlist of molecules, each with its evidence (a reported test result or a statement in a paper) linked to the exact passage. Your team keeps or rejects each one, and the kept molecules are exported for the simulation stage. We will use public sources only, subject to a licence check, and will flag approved drugs to help with repurposing.

The pilot will not include patents, preprints, conference posters or your internal reports. We will propose a way to add your reports later without your data leaving your environment. The pilot also won't judge which molecules will work; that stays with your scientists.

Before kickoff, ideally a week ahead, I need three decisions from you:

  1. Which protein to use, with the 20 to 30 molecules your team knows work on it, including some recent ones.
  2. Which scientist will test the pilot and sign off the results with you.
  3. Whether the protein name may leave your network.

For the third, a short call with your IT team should be enough.

We will agree success measures with you up front. I will share a one-page pilot plan for sign-off in week one, and a short update every Friday.

Task 4

Scenario: Three Weeks In

1.What I would do in the next 24 hours2.How I would work with engineering3.Options I would present to the client4.Protecting the date without hurting credibility

It is the end of week 3. The backend engineer says full-text papers will take two more weeks. That is the source that matters most: without it, we will probably miss measure 1b (finding molecules not already in ChEMBL), because abstracts often leave out the lab results.

1.What I would do in the next 24 hours

  • Same day, check the estimate: go through it task by task with BE, ML1 and the architect: done, left, unknown. Is the two weeks known work or uncertainty? Is something outside the team blocking us, like download limits? The architect gives a separate estimate, and ML2 runs measure 1 on what we have, so I know the real gap. Search, ChEMBL results, review screens and export don't depend on full papers, so the rest of the plan is safe.
  • Same day, agree with the CTO: the CTO wants to cut scope, and I agree, but we cut the right thing. The automatic pipeline moves out of the pilot; full papers for the pilot protein stay in. We agree the fallback (curated data plus abstracts) and go to the client with one recommendation.
  • Within 24 hours, call the VP, instead of waiting for the Friday email, with the options and my recommendation.

2.How I would work with engineering

I separate what the demo needs from what the finished product needs. The demo needs real full-text papers for one protein. The product needs a pipeline that updates automatically. So I ask: can we load the free papers for the pilot protein once, through the same connection, and build the automatic version after the pilot? If that is about a week of work, I pay for it by dropping BindingDB and screen polish, which were already first on the cut list. What we won't do: skip the quote check, skip accuracy testing, use unlicensed sources, or present a one-time load as a live connection.

3.Options I would present to the client

I recommend B, with A agreed in advance as the backup. The VP keeps the date, the CTO gets a smaller scope, and the demo shows real results.

4.Protecting the date without hurting credibility

  • Freeze new requests until the demo, and check the load every day.
  • Rehearse with the Senior Scientist in week 5.
  • At the demo, say plainly what is live, what was loaded once (with the date and number of papers), and when the automatic version arrives.
  • Brief the VP before the senior management session, so nothing surprises them, and report every measure as it is, even if one falls short.
