1.1The problem, and what we are proposing

Today, when a researcher picks a protein, they search databases and read through a large number of papers to find molecules that have been shown to act on it. It takes weeks, the result depends on who did the reading, and things get missed.

The VP wants this to be faster: "type in a protein and get the best molecules back". In the same call, they said they don't trust AI summaries and want their team to see the evidence and decide. I read those two statements together. The tool should not decide which molecule is best. It should do the slow part, finding and organising the evidence, and leave the judgement to the scientist.

What we propose: the researcher enters a protein and gets a shortlist of molecules. Next to each molecule is the evidence for it, with a link to the exact sentence or table it came from. The researcher reviews the list, keeps the good ones, and sends them on to the simulation stage.

The client calls this "protein–ligand matching", our engineers say "target–hit matching", and our sales deck says "AI-powered literature review". They describe the same job. I would use the client's term with the client and avoid "AI-powered literature review" in this pilot, because it sounds like the AI summaries the VP said they don't trust.

1.2Who uses it and what they see

Main user: the client's Senior Scientist and one or two colleagues working on the pilot protein. Also involved: the VP (sponsor) and whoever runs the simulation stage. I haven't assumed Toystack already has a simulation tool; confirming who owns that step is a week-1 question.

What the researcher does:

  1. Types the protein name. The tool shows the exact protein it found (for example, the human version, or a specific mutation) and asks them to confirm. Protein names are often ambiguous, and a wrong protein means wrong results.
  2. Gets a shortlist. Each row shows the molecule, how strongly it was reported to act on the protein, how many sources support it, how recent the evidence is, and whether it is already an approved drug. The Senior Scientist asked about repurposing approved drugs, and this flag covers that.
  3. Clicks a molecule to see each piece of evidence: the sentence or table row, the paper, and whether it came from a curated database or was found by the AI.
  4. Marks each molecule keep, reject or unsure, with a short note.
  5. Exports the kept molecules for the simulation stage.

What counts as a good match. I would sort results by how strong the evidence is, not by an AI score:

  • Strong: a paper or database reports a measured lab result for the molecule against this protein.
  • Medium: a paper says the molecule acts on the protein, but gives no number.
  • Weak: the two are only mentioned together. We keep these, but hide them by default.

Lab results are only compared when they come from the same kind of test, because numbers from different tests aren't comparable. The scientist can see exactly why each molecule sits where it does.

One honest limitation. Chemistry papers often call a molecule "compound 12b" and only show its structure in a drawing. The first version can't read drawings, so these go to a separate "needs checking" list instead of being guessed.

Who does what. Finding papers is automated. Pulling out the facts is done by AI, but every fact shows where it came from. Deciding whether the evidence is good enough, and whether a molecule is worth simulating, stays with the scientist.

What gets handed to simulation. A file listing each kept molecule's chemical structure, its reported result, links to its evidence and the scientist's decision, plus a reference to the protein's 3D structure, which simulation needs. I wouldn't guess the format. In week 1, I would agree it with whoever runs the simulation step. If there is no simulation tool yet, we check the file against a standard chemistry file format that common simulation tools read.

1.3Data sources

Two notes decide this. Finance says data must be free or licensed for commercial use, with no budget for paid licences. Engineering wants to scrape everything and sort out licensing later. I would follow Finance. A US pharma client will ask where every result came from, and being free to read online is not the same as being free to use commercially. Each licence was checked on the source's own website on 3 November 2026 (links at the end), with AI help; our lawyer should confirm them before we rely on them.

Not using: Google Scholar has no official API, and Google's terms forbid automated searching [14]. DrugBank's free version is for non-commercial use only, and commercial use needs a paid licence [13]; ChEMBL's approved-drug flag covers repurposing for the pilot. Paywalled papers can't be scraped. Conference posters have no reliable public source; the client's own posters can come in later with their internal documents.

What I'm not sure about. Two questions need a lawyer's answer by the end of week 1: can the AI read and quote short parts of copyrighted abstracts, and what does ChEMBL's "share-alike" condition mean if Toystack later sells this as a product? Until we have answers, we take the cautious option. If that means fewer results, we say so.

1.4How it fits together

The researcher confirms the protein (1). We collect what is known about it from the databases and papers (2, 3). The AI reads the relevant passages and records the facts in a fixed form: molecule, result and the exact quote (4). If the quote can't be found word for word in the paper, the item is thrown out, so the AI can't invent evidence. It could still attach a real sentence to the wrong molecule, which is why we measure accuracy (Task 2). We then match different names for the same molecule (5), store every item with its source and licence (6), and show the shortlist for review (7) before export (8).

Each source plugs in separately. Adding a new one later, such as patents or the client's own reports, doesn't mean rebuilding everything. That is how this can grow towards "any lab, any protein".

Keeping client data safe. The pilot uses only public data, so no internal client documents are involved. I would still treat the client's choice of protein and their searches as confidential, because the protein a pharma company is working on is sensitive in itself. The pilot would run in a separate space for this client, open only to named users, with the AI model set up so it doesn't keep or learn from what we send it. If the client's IT team says even the protein name can't leave their network, we would need to run inside their systems, and that approval can take longer than six weeks. So I would ask this before we start. If needed, the team builds on a public example protein while the approval runs. Internal reports come in a later phase, processed inside the client's own systems: public data comes in, their data never goes out.

1.5What we build first

Week 1, before building. Confirm the protein. Ask the Senior Scientist for 20–30 molecules they already know work on it (our "test list"). At least a third should be ones not yet in ChEMBL, for example from recent papers, so we are testing whether the AI finds anything new and not just whether ChEMBL works. Agree how we will measure success, agree the export format, and get the lawyer's answers.

First build: one protein, start to finish. Confirm the protein, pull ChEMBL results, search papers, run the AI with the quote check, show a simple shortlist, allow keep or reject, and export. By the end of week 2, a researcher should be able to type the protein and click from any result to its source. The screens will be rough, but the data will be real. I would build this thin version first because it answers the biggest unknown early: can the AI find real evidence beyond what databases already hold, accurately enough for scientists to trust it? Other sources can be added later.

Not in the first six weeks: patents, preprints, internal documents, posters, searching many proteins at once, chat or AI summaries, any AI verdict on whether a molecule is a good drug, and a direct connection to a simulation system (a file is enough for now).

How we will know it works: the five measures in Task 2, checked against the client's own test list.

2.2What we depend on

  • Client: protein and test list by day 5; about 3 hours a week from the Senior Scientist; two testers in week 5; an answer from IT on hosting before we start.
  • Data: enough free full-text papers exist for the chosen protein. We check this in week 1.
  • Team: the two ML engineers are our tightest resource, so they get no side work. The backend engineer is fully loaded in weeks 2–3, which is why BindingDB is optional. The architect's days are booked in advance.

2.3How I will track it

Weekly sprints with a Friday demo; the Senior Scientist joins from week 2. A 15-minute stand-up each morning. One shared list of risks, open issues and client dependencies, each with an owner and a date. A Friday email to the VP: on track or at risk, what's done, latest numbers, decisions needed. A short call with the VP and CTO every two weeks.

Early warning signs: the first version isn't working by the end of week 2; accuracy is below 80% in week 3; a client input is more than two days late; work is two days behind plan. If any of these happens, I tell the CTO the same day and the VP within 24 hours, with options.

These are my starting proposals. We agree and freeze them with the VP in week 1, before building.

Why 150 results for accuracy: it gives a 90% score a margin of about 5 points either way, without taking too much of the scientists' time.

Demo and sign-off. At the demo, a client scientist runs the tool live, not us. Then we show all five numbers, including any we missed. FE and I test the screens, ML2 runs the accuracy checks, and the client scientists do the final testing on real work. The VP signs off against the agreed measures, and the CTO signs off internally. If we miss a target, we say so, with the reason and a plan to fix it.

2.5If we run short on time or people

Never cut: links to sources, accuracy checks, the one-protein end-to-end flow, and full-text papers (measure 1b depends on them).

Cut first, in this order: BindingDB, then screen polish and extra filters, then the approved-drug filter.

Phase 2 regardless: patents, preprints, internal documents, several proteins at once, retraction information, user roles, and a direct connection to simulation.

Task 4

Scenario: Three Weeks In

It is the end of week 3. The backend engineer tells me that getting full-text papers working properly will take two more weeks. In my plan, that is the source that matters most: without full papers, we will probably miss measure 1b (finding molecules not already in ChEMBL), because abstracts often leave out the lab results.

What I check first (same day)

I sit down with BE, ML1 and the architect and go through the estimate task by task: what's done, what's left, and what we don't know yet. Is the two weeks known work, or uncertainty, for example reading tables we haven't tested yet? Is something outside the team blocking us, like download limits or access? I ask the architect for a separate estimate, and ML2 runs measure 1 on what we have today, so I know the real gap. Search, ChEMBL results, the review screens and export don't depend on full papers, so the rest of the plan is safe.

How I speak with engineering

I separate what the demo needs from what the finished product needs. The demo needs real full-text papers for one protein. The finished product needs a pipeline that updates automatically. So I ask: can we load the free papers for the pilot protein once, through the same connection, and build the automatic version after the pilot? The team checks how many papers that is the same day. If it is about a week of work, I fund it by dropping BindingDB and screen polish, which were already first on my cut list. I am also clear about what we won't do: skip the quote check, skip accuracy testing, use unlicensed sources, or present a one-time load as a live connection.

What I discuss with the CTO

The CTO wants to cut scope, and I agree, but I want to cut the right thing. My proposal: the automatic pipeline moves out of the pilot, and full papers for the pilot protein stay in. That is a real reduction, it protects the result the client cares about most, and we pay for it from the cut list. We also agree the fallback if the one-time load fails: curated data plus abstracts. Then we go to the client together with one recommendation.

Option for the clientWhat they get by the demoDownside

B. Keep the date, one-time load (recommended)Real full papers for the pilot protein; automatic updates two weeks after the demoNot automatic yet, and we say so; some risk, with A as the backup

My recommendation is B, with A agreed in advance as the backup. We decide on Friday of week 4: if the load is done and AI accuracy on it is at least 85%, we go with B. The VP keeps the date, the CTO gets a smaller scope, and the demo shows real results.

Protecting the date without false promises

I call the VP within 24 hours rather than waiting for the Friday email. I freeze new requests until the demo, check the load every day, and rehearse with the Senior Scientist in week 5. At the demo, we say plainly what is live, what was loaded once (with the date and number of papers), and when the automatic version arrives. I brief the VP before the senior management session so nothing surprises them in front of their leadership, and we report every measure as it is, even if one falls short.
